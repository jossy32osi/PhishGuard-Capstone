# PhishGuard: Phishing Awareness Guide

## 1. Introduction

Phishing is a form of social engineering in which an attacker impersonates a trusted person, organization, or service to deceive someone into taking an unsafe action. These actions may include clicking a suspicious link, opening an unexpected attachment, providing sensitive information, or sending money.

Phishing can appear through email, text messages, social media, websites, phone calls, and other communication channels.

The purpose of this guide is to help users recognize common phishing indicators and develop safer habits when dealing with unexpected or suspicious communications.

---

## 2. Why Phishing Matters

Phishing attacks are effective because they target human decision-making as well as technology. Attackers may create a sense of urgency, fear, curiosity, authority, or excitement to encourage people to act before carefully checking a message.

A phishing message may appear to come from a familiar organization or person. However, the message may contain warning signs that reveal that it is not trustworthy.

Recognizing these warning signs can help prevent unauthorized access, financial loss, identity theft, malware infections, and compromise of personal or organizational accounts.

---

## 3. Common Types of Phishing

### 3.1 Email Phishing

Email phishing uses deceptive emails to persuade recipients to click links, open attachments, provide information, or take another unsafe action.

### 3.2 Smishing

Smishing is phishing conducted through SMS or other text messages. A message may claim that a delivery, account, payment, or service requires immediate attention.

### 3.3 Vishing

Vishing is phishing conducted through voice communication. The attacker may impersonate a bank, company, technical support representative, government organization, or another trusted party.

### 3.4 Spear Phishing

Spear phishing is a targeted form of phishing directed at a particular individual or organization. The attacker may use information about the target to make the message appear more believable.

### 3.5 Business Email Compromise

Business Email Compromise involves fraudulent messages designed to appear as though they come from an executive, colleague, supplier, or other trusted business contact. The attacker may attempt to influence the recipient into making a payment or sharing sensitive information.

### 3.6 QR-Code Phishing

QR-code phishing uses QR codes to direct users toward fraudulent websites or other unsafe destinations. Because the destination may not be immediately visible, users should be cautious when scanning unexpected QR codes.

## 4. Common Warning Signs

A suspicious message may contain one or more of the following indicators:

### Unexpected Message

The message comes from someone or an organization you were not expecting to hear from.

### Urgent Language

The message pressures you to act immediately.

Examples include:

* "Your account will be closed today."
* "Immediate action required."
* "You have only 30 minutes."
* "Your payment has failed."

Urgency is often used to reduce the amount of time a person has to think and verify the request.

### Suspicious Sender

The sender's address, phone number, or account may not match the organization or person they claim to represent.

Be especially careful with addresses containing unexpected spelling changes or unusual domains.

### Suspicious Links

A message may contain a link that does not lead to the expected website.

Instead of clicking an unexpected link, navigate to the organization's official website using a trusted method.

### Requests for Sensitive Information

Be cautious when an unexpected message asks for:

* Passwords
* One-time verification codes
* Banking information
* Payment details
* Personal identification information
* Security answers

### Unexpected Attachments

Unexpected attachments can create security risks. Do not open an attachment simply because the message claims it is urgent or important.

### Unusual Grammar or Formatting

Spelling, grammar, formatting, or branding may sometimes reveal that a message is suspicious.

However, good grammar does **not** prove that a message is legitimate. Modern phishing messages can be professionally written.

### Emotional Pressure

The message may attempt to trigger:

* Fear
* Curiosity
* Excitement
* Panic
* Trust
* Authority

The goal is to make the recipient react instead of verify.

## 5. The STOP–CHECK–REPORT Method

PhishGuard uses a simple three-stage approach.

### STOP

Do not immediately click, reply, download, pay, or provide information.

Give yourself time to think.

### CHECK

Ask:

1. Who sent the message?
2. Was I expecting it?
3. What is the sender asking me to do?
4. Is the request unusual?
5. Is there urgency or emotional pressure?
6. Can I verify the request through an independent trusted channel?

If the message claims to come from an organization, use a website, phone number, or other contact method that you already know is legitimate rather than relying on the information contained in the suspicious message.

### REPORT

Follow your organization's or school's reporting procedure for suspicious messages.

After reporting, follow the appropriate local guidance for handling or deleting the message.

---

## 6. What To Do When You Receive a Suspicious Message

If you receive a suspicious email, text, or other communication:

1. Do not click unexpected links.
2. Do not open unexpected attachments.
3. Do not provide passwords or sensitive information.
4. Do not reply to the suspicious message.
5. Verify the request through a trusted independent channel.
6. Report the message using the appropriate reporting process.
7. Follow your organization's instructions for removing or retaining the message.

The FTC similarly recommends avoiding unexpected links and attachments and contacting organizations through information known to be genuine.

---

## 7. What If You Already Interacted With a Phishing Message?

Do not panic.

If you clicked a suspicious link, opened an unexpected attachment, or provided information:

1. Stop interacting with the message.
2. Tell a trusted adult, teacher, supervisor, or appropriate security contact.
3. Follow the organization's incident-reporting procedure.
4. If an account may have been compromised, follow the account provider's recovery and security guidance.
5. If instructed by a responsible security professional, perform the appropriate security checks on the device.

Reporting an incident quickly can help reduce potential harm and allow the appropriate people to respond.

---

## 8. Protecting Your Accounts

Good security habits can reduce the impact of phishing.

### Use Multi-Factor Authentication

Multi-factor authentication adds another verification step to an account. It can provide additional protection if a password is compromised.

### Keep Software Updated

Keep operating systems, applications, browsers, and security software updated.

### Use Strong, Unique Passwords

Avoid reusing the same password across important accounts.

### Be Careful With Unexpected Requests

Even if a message appears to come from someone you know, independently verify unusual requests involving money, credentials, sensitive information, or account changes.

---

## 9. Three Questions to Ask

Before acting on an unexpected message, ask:

### WHO?

Who is really sending this?

### WHAT?

What exactly are they asking me to do?

### WHY?

Why do they need me to do this now?

If the request does not make sense, stop and verify it.

---

## 10. PhishGuard Golden Rules

> **STOP before you click.**

> **CHECK before you trust.**

> **VERIFY before you provide information.**

> **REPORT when something looks suspicious.**

> **When in doubt, ask for help.**

---

## 11. Key Takeaway

Phishing prevention is not about being able to identify every scam perfectly. It is about developing habits that make unsafe actions less likely.

Slow down.

Check the sender.

Question unexpected requests.

Avoid suspicious links and attachments.

Verify through trusted channels.

Report suspicious activity.

**Think before you click.**
